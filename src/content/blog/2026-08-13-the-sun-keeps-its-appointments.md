Bottom line up front: last Wednesday the moon kept an appointment made before any of us were born, and the same week Washington proved it cannot keep the rules of money or justice fixed for even one news cycle. A free country needs its fixed laws where they matter most. We have put ours in the wrong places.

On August 12 the moon's shadow ran across Greenland, Iceland, Portugal and Spain. First total eclipse over Spain in more than a century. Iceland had not seen one since 1954, and Reykjavik not since 1433. Millions traveled to stand in the path, and here is the detail that should stop you: nobody doubted. Astronomers named the day, the minute, the second, generations ago, and no committee could revise it, no executive could delay it for political convenience. People booked thousand-dollar hotel rooms in Reykjavik on the strength of pure predictability. That is what fixed law does. It lets strangers plan together.

I am an engineer by training. At Purdue they taught me that Maxwell's equations do not care who holds office. Later I spent thirty-two years in Air Force, where navigation, timing, orbits, all of it rides on the stubborn regularity of creation. Jeremiah saw this long before the physicists. When God wanted to swear the strongest oath He could, He pointed at the sky: if you can break my covenant with day and night, with the fixed order of heaven and earth, then my promises can fail too. Fixed order is not poetry decoration. It is the ground trust stands on.

Now set that against the week's other two events.

On August 7 the White House sent Federal Reserve governor Lisa Cook a letter, signed by the deputy chief of staff, telling her the president is considering removing her and giving her three weeks to answer mortgage-fraud allegations no court has established. The Supreme Court already blocked one attempt to fire her, back in June. The president already has his own man, Kevin Warsh, in the chairman's seat since May. So this fight is not really about Cook's mortgages. It is about whether the money three hundred million strangers use to plan their lives runs on rules or runs on will.

Austrians have said this for a hundred years: the deepest harm of discretionary money is not inflation alone, it is unpredictability. Mises and Hayek argued that what separates law from command is that law binds the ruler too. I did not learn this from books first. I bought bitcoin in 2011 because twenty-one million was a number no chairman could revise. An eclipse schedule for money. Then Mt. Gox taught me the other half of the lesson, the expensive half: the protocol never broke, my custodian did. Fixed rules failed me nowhere. Trusted men failed me completely. Washington has been confusing those two failures ever since, blaming rules for what discretion did.

Then Saturday, August 8, the Senate confirmed Todd Blanche as attorney general, 50 to 49. The president's own criminal defense lawyer is now the nation's chief law enforcement officer, by a single vote. Maybe he surprises everyone. But notice what the arrangement itself says. For thirty-two years I swore my oath, several times over, to the Constitution, never to a commander in chief. That distinction is the whole American invention. My parents' generation in Taiwan lived under the other model, where law was whatever the ruling party needed it to be that week. Taiwan did not become free and prosperous because better men took power. It became free when rules grew strong enough that it stopped mattering so much who took power.

That is the thread connecting an eclipse, a threatening letter, and a one-vote confirmation. Every civilization decides where its fixed laws live. Creation's laws are non-negotiable, so we build GPS and eclipse tourism on top of them without a second thought. Money and justice are human institutions, so their fixedness is a choice, renewed or abandoned by each generation. This administration is abandoning it in both places at once, and many who cheer today will be first to scream when a president they despise inherits all that discretion.

The Christian has extra reason to care. We serve a God who binds Himself, who covenants, who points at the fixed order of heaven as proof His word holds. A ruler who recognizes no rule above his own will is imitating a different god.

Last Wednesday in Spain the sun kept its appointment to the second, and millions of strangers trusted it enough to gather under it. Ask yourself which institution of American government you would trust that far. Then ask what it would take to build one. Start with rules that bind the ruler. Everything else follows.
